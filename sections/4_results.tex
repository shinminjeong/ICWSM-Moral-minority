\textbf{Minority judgments received lower scores.} As shown in Figure~\ref{fig:engagement-by-consensus}, in AITA, the mean score was $37.95$ for majority comments and $-2.75$ for minority comments; in AITAH, the corresponding means were $14.30$ and $-0.19$. This score gap became considerably smaller in controversial posts. 

\textbf{Minority judgments attracted more direct replies.} As shown in Figure~\ref{fig:engagement-by-consensus}, in non-controversial posts, minority comments received an average of $0.53$ direct replies in AITA, compared with $0.14$ for majority comments. A similar pattern appeared in AITAH, where minority and majority comments received $0.38$ and $0.12$ replies, respectively. Under controversy, however, this engagement advantage diminished: the corresponding means were $0.30$ versus $0.26$ in AITA and both were $0.21$ in AITAH. Thus, minority judgments generated greater discussion primarily when they challenged an otherwise strong group consensus.

\textbf{Minority judgments generated deeper reply threads.}
As shown in Figure~\ref{fig:reply-depth-comparison}, in non-controversial AITA posts, minority comments had a mean reply depth of $0.82$, compared with $0.13$ for majority comments; $15.7\%$ of minority threads reached a depth of at least two, compared with $2.5\%$ of majority threads. A similar pattern appeared in AITAH: minority and majority comments had mean reply depths of $6.50$ and $0.12$, respectively, while $12.1\%$ and $2.0\%$ reached a depth of at least two. These differences narrowed substantially in controversial posts. Overall, minority judgments stimulated deeper discussion mainly when they challenged an otherwise strong group consensus.

\textbf{Comment engagement was highest for early comments and quickly decreased over time.} As shown in Figure~\ref{fig:comment-engagement-over-time}, in both AITA and AITAH, comments posted during the first hour received the highest scores and the greatest number of direct replies. Under strong consensus, minority comments generally received lower scores than majority comments but attracted more replies, particularly during the earliest hours. This minority engagement advantage weakened quickly as comments arrived later. The large differences between mean and median values further indicate that early engagement was highly skewed and driven partly by a small number of exceptionally visible comments.

\subsection{RQ3: The direction of dissent --- accusing versus defending}
\label{subsec:res-direction}

Figure~\ref{fig:dissent-direction} compares accusing and defending dissent
(\S\ref{subsec:method}) in $1{,}500$ sampled consensual high-volume posts from
each community. Each condition is shown against the majority comments of the
same post type, which are not themselves classified by direction.

\paragraph{Voting disfavors the minority in both directions.}
Across both communities, minority comments received markedly lower scores than
the majority comments of the same post type. In AITA, accusing minority
comments had a mean score of $-3.2$ against $37.2$ for majority comments in
NA-dominant posts, and defending minority comments $-2.4$ against $35.4$ in
YA-dominant posts. AITAH shows the same ordering on a compressed scale:
accusing minority comments averaged $-0.6$ against $14.7$, and defending
minority comments $1.1$ against $11.2$. The one condition in which dissent
attracts net approval---defending dissent in AITAH---still falls an order of
magnitude below the majority it is measured against, so the gap is a matter of
degree rather than of sign.

\paragraph{Replies move in the opposite direction.}
In AITA, $29.3\%$ of accusing and $17.2\%$ of defending minority comments drew
more than one reply, against only $4.6\%$ and $4.1\%$ of their respective
majority baselines. AITAH repeats the asymmetry: $22.7\%$ and $12.8\%$ for
minority comments against $4.1\%$ for both majority baselines. The baselines
are the informative part here. Majority comments are answered at an almost
constant low rate---between $4.1\%$ and $4.6\%$ regardless of post type or
community---while the minority rate varies by a factor of three across the same
cells. Whatever governs how much reply a comment attracts operates almost
entirely on dissent.

\paragraph{Accusing draws more discussion than defending.}
Within the minority, the accusing direction is consistently the more engaged
one. It exceeds defending dissent by a factor of $1.70$ in AITA and $1.77$ in
AITAH on the reply measure, and by $1.87$ and $1.83$ on reply depth, where
$17.8\%$ of accusing against $9.5\%$ of defending comments in AITA, and
$13.2\%$ against $7.2\%$ in AITAH, seed subtrees deeper than two levels. The
absolute rates differ by roughly a third between the two communities, yet every
one of these ratios falls between $1.7$ and $1.9$.

Taken together, these descriptive patterns separate approval from
conversational attention. Minority judgments were strongly disfavored in voting
relative to majority judgments, yet were disproportionately likely to initiate
further discussion, and the contrast is sharpest for accusing dissent. Sanction
and attention therefore do not track a single underlying severity: the direction
that is voted down hardest is also the direction that is answered most.

\subsection{RQ4: AITA/AITAH + timeline}
\paragraph{Certainty change over time
18h rule.}
Figure~\ref{fig:certainty-time-series} shows the mean certainty expressed by majority- and minority-side comments during the first 25 hours after submission.
Majority-side comments consistently express greater certainty than minority-side comments in both communities. After the 18-hour threshold, minority certainty increases in AITA but decreases temporarily in AITAH.

\paragraph{Minority verdict
ratio over time.}
Minority participation develops differently depending on whether the minority accuses or defends the OP. In both communities, accusing minorities in NA-dominant posts remain relatively stable or gradually increase over time. By contrast, defending minorities in YA-dominant posts follow different trajectories: their cumulative share rises slightly in AITA but declines sharply in AITAH, from approximately 11\% to 7\%. Thus, the strongest temporal change occurs among commenters who defend an OP condemned by the majority, particularly in AITAH.
